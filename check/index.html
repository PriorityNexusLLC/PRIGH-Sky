    Method
    
    
    All decent questions to complex answers, not for judgement or coercion, only clarity: a big complex picture drawn into a legible map.
    
    
    This check asks nine questions of any rule a person lives under. It does not decide who is good or bad. It asks three things: does the rule leave people in charge of themselves, can it be seen clearly, and does it treat everyone it touches fairly.
    
    
    How the answers are made
    
    
    
      
    1
    Collect
    
        
    Programs download the official law in full, straight from the government: the whole U.S. Code and the whole eCFR (federal rules).
    
        15,735chapters and parts read so far
    
      
    2
    Weigh
    
        
    For each gate, the program finds the sentences that answer it, counts them, and checks who can act: the person, or only someone else. It follows the laws a law points to, one step out.
    
        15,979laws you can search on the Check page
    
      
    3
    Write in
    
        
    The program writes its answer into each gate, with the quote, the link and a plain-words version beside the original. A person reads it and can change any answer.
    
        1,427passages put in plain words
    
    
    
    
    Why we do this: friction
    
    
    Every moving system carries friction. Our goal is to protect the positive friction and set boundaries to the negative frictions in the way of sovereignty.
    
    
    We call what we protect the whimsy loop: freedom to be, to do, to choose, to learn, without the constant constraints of outside pressure and friction pushing against the three cores and nine gates.
    
    
    Friction is the pressure a rule puts on the people it touches. Every rule has some. The question is whether that pressure has a way out.
    
    
      
      
    • Positive friction has a way out. A rule that slows a decision so a person can be told, can answer, can appeal or can go to court puts pressure on everyone, and gives that pressure somewhere to go. That friction protects people, and we protect it.
      • 
      
    • Negative friction is pressure with no way out. The rule itself is not the problem. The problem is the door that should open and doesn't: no notice, no hearing, no appeal, no way to sue. That is where we look for boundaries.
      • 
    
    
    
    The nine gates measure this. Notice, a hearing, an appeal and a way to sue are the doors. When a gate shows none of them, the pressure on a person has nowhere to go, and the page says so with the words of the rule itself.
    
    
    How the answers work
    
    
      
      
    • Every answer points to words in the rule itself. An answer without a quote is an opinion, and the report shows it that way.
      • 
      
    • "Can't tell" is an honest answer. What the text leaves out is reported, not guessed.
      • 
      
    • The same nine questions apply to every rule and every author: a company, a government, a school, a church, a group, a person.
      • 
      
    • All nine answers are shown together. One "no" does not hide the rest.
      • 
      
    • The full report can be copied, so anyone can hold it against the original text and check it.
      • 
      
    • For laws Priority Nexus has read, the page shows the passages our program found for each gate, with the count and a link to the law. A program finds words, not meaning: a passage can say the opposite of what the gate hopes for. The person answering reads it and decides.
      • 
      
    • Under every gate are links to official public records where the answer can be looked up.
      • 
      
    • Every gate asks the same thing: can people do it for themselves? A door only counts if a person can use it. A hearing for the company being fined, or the government's right to sue, is not a door for the people. When only an agency, an official or a lawyer can act, the page says "only through" and names them, because that shows who speaks for people, who decides for them, and who keeps them from deciding for themselves.
      • 
      
    • For the doors (notice, a hearing, a way to court, seeing the record, the law reporting on itself, and a person's own right to sue), the answer reads the whole: the law itself and the laws it points to. To keep that honest, the program goes one step out only, follows only citations that land on an exact chapter or part it has read, and names the law, sentence and link for every door it finds elsewhere. A law's own ban on court review always comes first. Every law it points to is listed with a link, with how many times it is cited.
      • 
      
    • "Not written in the part we read" means the program did not find it in the part of the law it read. The law may not have it, it may sit in another law, or it may be worded in a way the search misses. For federal laws, a general law, the Administrative Procedure Act (5 U.S.C. 702), gives court review of most agency actions even when the law itself says nothing. The page says when an answer rests on that general law, and when a law bars court review of some of its decisions.
      • 
      
    • Each passage our program found also has a plain-words version, written with AI under fixed rules: every person, action, condition and "unless" is kept; nothing is added; "shall" becomes "must" and every "not" stays. The original always sits right above it, so anyone can check one against the other.
      • 
    
    
    
    What it does not do
    
    
    It does not judge what anyone intended. It does not tell you what to do. It asks simple questions of sovereignty and shows where the answers came from.
